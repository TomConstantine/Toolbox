Method

Manual edges

Useful where vegetation or shadow hides the true edge and auto‑detection is unreliable — digitise it by eye instead. Use the map's edit tool (top‑right) to reshape or delete any line.

Baseline

Draw one line on the stable, landward (or otherwise fixed) side of the feature. Transects are cast perpendicular to it, DSAS‑style, and measured out to each edge line.

Transects

Needs: a baseline, plus at least one edge line tagged Survey A and one tagged Survey B.

Export

Canny + Otsu thresholding — the classic approach for pulling a coastline or cliff break‑line out of optical imagery (adaptive‑threshold Canny coastline extraction goes back to Liu & Jezek 2004, and the same combination shows up repeatedly in recent UAV cliff‑erosion work). Good for a sharp, bare‑earth scarp edge.

Excess Green (2G−R−B) + Otsu — a standard RGB‑only substitute for NDVI (no near‑infrared sensor needed, so it works with an ordinary drone camera). Traces the vegetation trim‑line, which is often a more stable, trackable proxy than the true bank toe when overhanging vegetation obscures it — the same trade‑off noted in UAV riverbank studies (e.g. the Buëch river monitoring work), which combined automated extraction on open banks with manual digitising under tree cover. That's why manual edge digitising is offered alongside the automatic methods here.

HSV Value channel + Otsu — water shows up as a patch of unusually uniform brightness, which is the basis of recent histogram‑based UAV river‑coastline detectors. Works best where the water's edge isn't hidden by overhanging vegetation.

Transect / DSAS method — the standard way shoreline and cliff‑top retreat is measured between two dated surveys (USGS's Digital Shoreline Analysis System): a baseline, regularly spaced perpendicular transects, and the distance from baseline to each dated edge line along every transect. Used this way in published cliff‑top retreat studies comparing historic and recent orthophotos.

Georeferencing note — pixel↔map coordinates are computed by linear interpolation across the raster's reprojected bounding box, not a full projected transform. This is accurate for the kind of unrotated, small‑extent orthomosaic a drone survey produces, but isn't appropriate for large or rotated mosaics spanning a wide area.
